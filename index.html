Call for papers

Security and privacy mechanisms are used by everyone, but they are usually designed and evaluated for a narrow group of users.

Authentication mechanisms mostly assume that a person can see a screen, hold a device steadily, remember a code and receive a message on another device. Consent dialogues, permission prompts and security warnings mostly assume that all users have a particular level of technical knowledge, can quickly read dense text and can understand every instruction.

These assumptions do not hold for everyone, and they can weaken security and privacy for people with visual or hearing impairments, people with motor or cognitive disabilities, neurodivergent people, older adults, children and young people, people with limited digital literacy and people using a second language. These users may stop using security features, share their credentials or devices, or avoid recommended security practices because they feel these technologies were not designed with their needs in mind.

This also creates serious risks for people experiencing technology-facilitated abuse, where access to devices, accounts and personal data can be used to control, monitor or harm them. Now that all major sectors, including banking, healthcare, public services and entertainment, have moved online, security controls that do not cater for the accessibility and inclusion needs of these groups result in real security and privacy compromises. Designing security and privacy technologies that recognise the needs and experiences of these groups can make them more useful, accessible and inclusive.

CyberAccess welcomes research that treats inclusion and accessibility as key requirements of security and privacy engineering. We are interested in the whole journey: from understanding people’s needs and experiences, to designing, building and evaluating security and privacy technologies, to standards and policy. The main objective of CyberAccess is to understand what can be changed to make security and privacy technologies more inclusive, accessible and safe, without compromising security and privacy.

Topics of interest

CyberAccess welcomes research on a broad range of topics, including but not limited to:

  • Threat modelling and security for people with accessibility needs
  • Security and privacy of assistive technologies
  • Security, privacy and inclusion across cultural, linguistic and socioeconomic contexts
  • Trust, risk perception and security decision-making among diverse users
  • Methods and benchmarks for evaluating accessible and inclusive cybersecurity
  • Large language models and AI for accessible and inclusive security
  • Ethical and inclusive research methods in cybersecurity
  • Privacy and safety of technologies used by children and young people
  • Technology-facilitated abuse, and security and privacy in FemTech
  • Security and privacy for people with motor or cognitive disabilities
  • Security and privacy for people with physical, sensory or perceptual disabilities

Submission

Papers must be written in English and formatted using the Springer LNCS template. The page limit, review policy and submission link will be announced here soon.

  • Accepted papers will appear in the ACNS 2027 workshop post-proceedings, published by Springer in the Lecture Notes in Computer Science (LNCS) series.
  • Presentations must be given in person at the workshop.
  • At least one author of each accepted paper must register for ACNS 2027 at the author rate.

Best Workshop Paper Award. Papers accepted at CyberAccess are eligible for the ACNS 2027 Best Workshop Paper Award, a EUR 500 prize sponsored by Springer and selected from all ACNS workshops.

Programme chairs

Dr Waleed Bin Shahid

Lecturer, Information Security Group, Department of Computing, Security and Mathematics, Royal Holloway, University of London, UK

Waleed is a systems security researcher whose work also focuses on inclusivity in cybersecurity. He is interested in understanding the weaknesses and limitations of security systems and software for people with different abilities, including neurodivergent people. He also explores how LLMs and AI can be used to build more secure and inclusive systems that better consider the needs of people with different abilities.

Dr Maryam Mehrnezhad

Reader, Information Security Group, Department of Computing, Security and Mathematics, Royal Holloway, University of London, UK

Maryam’s research focuses on cyber safety, security and privacy, with a keen focus on real-world issues. She studies the security and privacy risks of emerging technologies, including IoT devices, smart cameras and health technologies. Her work aims to protect people at risk, including children, people with disabilities, and victims and survivors of technology-facilitated abuse, and to help them have better control over their data through accessible, ethical and secure technologies.
